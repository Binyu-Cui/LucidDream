# Week 13

### Labs

After going through this lab, I realized that Unity wraps up a lot of the complicated stuff involved in making games and XR experiences—like visuals, audio, and hardware integration—into a more manageable package. That should make development faster. But honestly, I’m still not totally clear on how all of that actually works under the hood.

The most frustrating part of the lab was dealing with the variety of XR devices—headsets, glasses, and so on. They all seem to work differently, and there doesn’t seem to be a consistent standard. The lab mentioned something called OpenXR that’s supposed to make things more unified, but in practice, just getting my device connected and running the project was expected to take 30 minutes—longer than everything else combined. It made me wonder: why is it this complicated? Does every device have its own issues?

The lab materials say Unity is the most commonly used tool for XR, and it has its own system for managing device connections. But apparently, Unity used to have its own solution and is now switching to OpenXR. Does that mean I’ll have to learn both approaches? That’s a bit concerning.

As for terms like VR (fully virtual), AR (adding virtual elements to the real world), and MR (mixing the two), the explanations were clear, but then it also said the XR field is still new and definitions aren’t fixed. So I’m left unsure how to decide which direction to take when starting a project.

There was an example in the lab of an artist using VR to make creative work. It was cool and inspiring, but I still don’t really know how to actually build something like that in Unity. The step-by-step part seems missing—from having an idea to making it real.

Overall, the lab showed me that Unity is trying to make XR development more accessible. But dealing with hardware is still a big pain, and just getting things connected can take up a lot of time. I’ve realized I need to keep an eye on standards like OpenXR and figure out which devices properly support it. More importantly, I’d really like clearer, more practical guidance on how to take creative ideas and actually build them in Unity—rather than just watching examples of what others have done.

### Reading & Reflections

While adjusting parameters in Unity, I suddenly understood what Merleau-Ponty meant by “the body is the medium of perception.” Every time I turned my head, the slight delay in the scene reminded me of how fragile the sense of bodily presence is in virtual space. True immersion doesn’t come from 360-degree textures—it comes from users being able to unquestioningly believe they can physically touch objects in the virtual world.

Lanier’s idea of the “disappearing computer” becomes concrete in Unity workflows. We constantly try to hide draw calls and reduce rendering load, similar to how painters used to hide seams in panoramic artworks. But what struck me most were the unintended effects: for example, in a forest scene, a user instinctively ducked to avoid a virtual branch. That reaction wasn’t guided—it came from muscle memory. This kind of bodily trick feels more real than any visual effect.

The contradictions in VR history are still present today. On one hand, we’re chasing Sensorama-style multi-sensory experiences; on the other, we’re still limited by headset weight and screen resolution. This is where Unity’s physics engine shows its strength: when a ball rolls down a slope, it’s not pre-animated—it’s calculated in real time. This kind of system-generated behavior gives the scene a stronger sense of presence—just like the material says, “a sense of reality comes from resistance to the will.”
![image](https://git.arts.ac.uk/24007733/Responsive-Environments-Blog-2024/assets/1326/41034f93-ba96-44fa-8fe7-26595db682a3)

The biggest gap between current VR and the idea of an “ultimate medium” is still the disconnect between bodily sensation and data. Maybe we’ll only get closer to a truly inhabitable virtual reality when Unity can simulate touch in a meaningful way—like the sensation of water flowing between your fingers.
