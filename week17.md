# Week 17

### Labs

This lab session made me rethink how I share the projects I'm working on. I used to believe it was better to wait until something was finished or polished before showing it to anyone. Now I see that sharing should be part of the process itself—not just the final step.

The idea of “making the process public” really stuck with me. Instead of waiting for a clean result, I could just share while I’m testing things—whether that’s code, drafts, or even failed attempts. It doesn’t matter if things go wrong. That kind of openness makes the work feel more real and might even lead to better feedback. Next time I do a test or prototype, I’m planning to document as I go and post short updates in the group chat.

I also started thinking more about *who* I’m sharing with. For technical parts, I could use GitHub so others with similar experience can follow or contribute. For more conceptual or cross-disciplinary ideas, I might try something like a digital garden—a public notebook where I collect fragments and connections for anyone interested to browse. As for final results or key insights, maybe they don’t have to be formal. A simple, visual summary—like a mini zine—could be more engaging and accessible.

For this lab specifically, I’m planning three things: first, I’ll post weekly updates on my progress, including things that didn’t work or are still unclear; second, I’ll open a discussion section in the project repo for feedback or questions; and third, I want to explore a more informal format for presenting the final outcome—something more visual and approachable than a standard report.

The main shift is letting go of the idea that I need to be “ready” before I share. Sharing earlier, even when things are rough, can make the process more collaborative, more interesting, and ultimately more valuable.

### Reading & Reflections

The session focused on research and writing within the context of “computational reality.” It offered a structured overview of current concerns—particularly the growing entanglement of physical and digital spaces and how they shape one another. A key point was that virtual experiences aren’t detached from physical reality; they rely heavily on material infrastructure and generate real-world effects. At the same time, digital connectivity is reshaping traditional ideas of space, leading to new hybrid spatial forms.

The introduction of the “computational regime” provided a useful framework for thinking about computation not just as a tool, but as a fundamental logic through which the world is interpreted and produced. That said, the session also acknowledged its limits as a cognitive model. The discussion on electronic textuality—how digital media transforms the structure, circulation, and reception of text—highlighted its departure from print logic and connected directly to newer developments like generative AI.

There was a strong emphasis on research methodology. The course argued for combining technical knowledge (code, algorithms, data) with experiential and interpretive knowledge (intuition, embodied experience, cultural reading). How users perceive and navigate digital space—through spatial awareness or procedural flow—was presented as essential to understanding human-computer interaction. The session also showed how historical practices (like stereoscopes or Dada poetry) can be read alongside contemporary ones (like immersive games or AI-generated media), revealing the historical continuity of digital forms.

It drew on interdisciplinary sources including spatial theory, game studies, and posthuman thought. One important point was the lack of clear understanding in the overlaps between these fields—particularly how computation materially reshapes human spatial experience and ways of being. The “& Statements” idea was introduced as a way to analyze key tensions and links: physical & digital, hard knowledge & soft knowledge. Conceptual models like Yamaguchi’s formula on virtuality were used to map out complex relationships.

Overall, the session laid out a clear conceptual and methodological approach to studying computational reality. It focused on how algorithms, data, and interfaces mediate and transform spatial perception, textual practices, and knowledge production. I found the themes and the research-oriented framing particularly engaging, and it gave me a solid foundation to think more critically about where my own work could go in this area.
