# Week 2

### Labs

I realized that game controllers are never neutral technical objects. That quote "Universal control assumes a universal body" really shows how every "standard" controller design quietly excludes certain bodies. When I hold a gamepad, this seemingly ordinary piece of plastic is actually reinforcing assumptions about what a "normal" body should be like.  
<img width="1045" alt="image" src="https://git.arts.ac.uk/24007733/Responsive-Environments-Blog-2024/assets/1326/75708437-761c-4f41-a498-21733d50f57c">


"By queering game controls and controllers, we can access more ways to question, transform, resist, imagine, and bring difference to game design" - this offers a new perspective. I'm starting to understand that designing controllers can be a form of resistance, a way to challenge established rules.  
<img width="999" alt="image" src="https://git.arts.ac.uk/24007733/Responsive-Environments-Blog-2024/assets/1326/3917d8ae-ddb1-4b4a-9fea-f9cb95a8229f">

When I think about what "queering" controllers means, I find it's not just about gender or sexual orientation, but about deviating from, twisting, and redirecting things we take for granted. The plant controller example is particularly inspiring - "let them interact with something other than plastic." This simple but powerful statement opens up possibilities: games controlled by breathing, controllers that need multiple people to operate, or even controllers that "resist" the player.

The concept of reflective design also redefines the goals of game design. "Disruption over comfort," "reflection over immersion" - these principles contrast with what mainstream game design pursues. I realize that making players uncomfortable, making them aware of their bodies, breaking immersion, might create deeper experiences. This kind of design isn't about helping players escape reality, but making them face reality more consciously. These theoretical frameworks provide a critical foundation for alternative controller design.

### Reading & Reflections

After watching Matt Jarvis's presentation on Arduino and alternative controllers, I was deeply moved by the phrase "Simple works!" In our age of pursuing complex technology, we often forget that the most touching interactions are often the simplest ones. Simon Blackmore used a single boat rudder to navigate internet radio stations, transforming digital browsing into a poetic sailing experience. This imagination contained within simplicity left a lasting impression on me.

The Hapti-Harness project converts GPS directions into vibrations on the body. This sensory translation reminded me of Neil Harbisson's Eyeborg. Born colorblind, this artist implanted an antenna in his head that converts color frequencies into sounds, allowing him to "hear" colors. These projects explore how technology can become new organs for perceiving the world, not just tools. When technology truly merges with the body and extends our senses, the relationship between humans and the world is redefined.  
![image](https://git.arts.ac.uk/24007733/Responsive-Environments-Blog-2024/assets/1326/34fd3209-f0c0-4746-9bc3-0bb47f67f9f5)  
*Neil Harbisson charging himselfup with electricity via a plug socket.*

"Team Untangle" and "Forest Daydream" showcase the charm of collaboration in physical computing. When multiple people must solve a spatial puzzle together, how does individual behavior affect the whole? This exploration of collective intelligence reminded me of Daily tous les jours' "21 Balançoires" - 21 interactive swings that form a musical instrument. Each swing corresponds to a musical note, and only when strangers swing together can they create harmonious melodies. This design cleverly combines playfulness, musicality, and social interaction.  
![image](https://git.arts.ac.uk/24007733/Responsive-Environments-Blog-2024/assets/1326/a84b8197-809b-4844-92cf-5bd2b861cb49)

Don Norman's design principles mentioned in the presentation become particularly interesting in the context of physical computing. When interaction breaks free from the two-dimensional limits of screens, "discoverability" becomes a spatial problem - how do users understand possible actions in three-dimensional environments? Touch, sound, and light all become media for conveying information. This multi-sensory design challenge pushes us beyond traditional interface thinking to create more intuitive, embodied interaction languages.

From dodgems to forest daydream, from personal sensory extensions to collective collaborative experiences, these projects remind me that physical computing isn't just about sensors and actuators. It's about using technology to weave new experiential possibilities. Every time an Arduino LED blinks in response to a sensor trigger, we're actually creating a small magical moment - a poetic instant that connects the physical and digital, body and environment.

### Peer Support

This week in class, we played each other's different games and shared our experiences making them. I felt really excited because I discovered that everyone's games were unique - both in gameplay and visual expression. While participating in each game, I experienced a kind of excitement I'd never felt before. Part of this excitement came from feeling how these games, despite being quite simple and not made with complex tools or software, were still filled with thoughtful little details. You could sense the design choices each creator had put into their work. Many classmates participated in our games and gave us diverse feedback, which helped us reflect more deeply on the games themselves.
