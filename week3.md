# Week 3

### Labs

The game design section emphasizes iterative development, which I think is crucial. Creative work is all about trial and error, and teaching us to refine their projects through feedback is way more useful than just chasing perfect first drafts.

As for the Unity intro, it’s clear that beginners face some real hurdles. The concepts—GameObjects, transforms, parent-child hierarchies—are explained well enough in slides, but actually applying them is another story. The 2D cat examples help visualize positioning, but making the jump to 3D thinking isn’t easy. And while the component-based system is powerful, it’s a big shift from traditional coding—beginners have to stop thinking in linear steps and start thinking in terms of objects and behaviors.

The terminology also comes fast: GameObjects, Components, Scripts, Scenes… it’s a lot to absorb at once. And setting up the workspace—choosing between Visual Studio and VS Code, picking the right build targets—adds another layer of complexity that new learners might not fully grasp at first. Still, there’s a noticeable gap between understanding the concepts and actually working in Unity’s interface. More hands-on practice would probably help bridge that.

### Reading & Reflections

This categorization of play types is insightful for framing game design discussions, though real-world games often blend these categories fluidly rather than adhering to strict boundaries. The emphasis on experience-based play as a distinct pillar strongly resonates with me—it validates emotionally driven games like Journey that prioritize immersion over traditional objectives. While competitive/cooperative dynamics are well-defined, I wish the framework acknowledged modern nuances like how streaming culture transforms performance play through audience interaction, or how metagaming (player-created systems beyond core rules) becomes its own play layer in communities. The exclusion of luck’s role in enhancing narrative tension (e.g., dramatic XCOM misses) feels like a missed opportunity in the chance/uncertainty section. Most compelling is the closing argument that "play expands beyond games"—a concept I’d extend to UX design, where cooperative mechanics or competitive elements can deepen engagement in non-gaming contexts. Ultimately, this taxonomy’s real value lies in recognizing play as fundamental to human interaction, not just entertainment.
